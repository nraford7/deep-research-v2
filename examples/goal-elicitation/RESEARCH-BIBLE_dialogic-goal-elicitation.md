# 1. Why Stated Goals Misrepresent Underlying Desires

The premise of any goal-elicitation practice is that the first goal a person states is a starting point, not a destination. The sources reviewed here offer several mechanisms by which stated goals can diverge from the goals that actually govern a person's satisfaction, persistence, and eventual judgment of success: action identification theory [Vallacher & Wegner, Psychological Review, 1987], goal systems theory [Kruglanski, New Developments in Goal Systems Theory, OUP, 2023], self-concordance research [Sheldon & Elliot, JPSP, 1999], conversation-analytic work on presupposition [Schoeb, Studies in Communication Sciences, 2014], and an engineering folklore tradition (the XY problem) that arrived at a similar description independently [Meta Stack Exchange, 2010]. How often such divergence occurs in practice is not established by these sources (see 1.3, Prevalence).

<!-- editorial:background -->
Field map. The mainstream position, shared across motivational psychology, therapeutic practice, and requirements engineering, is that stated goals are surface expressions shaped by what is cognitively present, socially safe, and easy to articulate, and that the underlying desire must be recovered by structured probing. Within that consensus the schools differ on whether the deep goal pre-exists and merely needs uncovering, or is partly constructed by the act of elicitation itself; and on whether repeated why-probing recovers stable underlying structure or manufactures plausible-sounding answers on demand. Heterodox positions visible in the corpus include the claim that for intrinsically motivated pursuits the means-versus-ends distinction is itself a category error, so decomposing the activity into "what you are doing" and "what it is for" destroys rather than clarifies; and the claim that even accurately recovered deep goals can be wrong, because people mispredict how attaining them will feel. Better supported, within the experimental domains the cited work actually studied: people shift toward stating means instead of ends when the task becomes difficult, and question phrasing with embedded assumptions distorts answers; the transfer of these findings to everyday help-seeking, including the claim that help-seekers anchor on an attempted solution, is inferential rather than measured. Contested: which mechanism dominates (cognitive, social, or structural); whether introspective reports of goal reasons are causal accounts or post-hoc constructions; and how often the misstatement actually changes the correct answer.
<!-- /editorial -->

## 1.1 Surface vs. deep goals: the psychological architecture

**Action identification theory** provides the corpus's most direct account of why stated goals sit at the wrong level. Any action can be identified at many levels, "ranging from low-level identities that specify how the action is performed to high-level identities that signify why or with what effect the action is performed"; the level a person adopts reflects a trade-off between comprehensive understanding and effective action maintenance, and the actor "moves to lower levels of identification if the action proves difficult to maintain with higher level identities in mind" [Vallacher & Wegner, Psychological Review, 1987]. Difficulty is also a typical condition under which people seek help, so the identity that is cognitively present at the moment of asking may be the low-level mechanical one ("get the last three characters of the filename") rather than the end ("get the file extension"). [confidence: inferential; the theory was not developed or tested on help-seeking] Contextual cues, conversely, invite migration back up to "why" framings [Vallacher & Wegner, Psychological Review, 1987]: the theoretical warrant for elicitation itself.

**Goal systems theory** formalizes the means-ends structure that stated goals compress. Goals and means form networks with equifinality (many means to one goal), multifinality (one means serving several goals), and counterfinality; attaching additional alternative means to a goal reduces the intensity of motivation toward any single means (the "more is less" effect) [Kruglanski, New Developments in Goal Systems Theory, OUP, 2023, citing Bélanger et al., JESP, 2015]. A stated goal is thus a single node extracted from a lattice; the same means can be stated in service of different deep goals, and the same deep goal can surface as many different stated means.

**The means-ends fusion model** supplies the structural caveat: intrinsic motivation is defined as "perceptual fusion between the activity and its purpose; these two are one," illustrated by Mallory's "because it is there". For such pursuits the person cannot separate pursuing the activity from receiving its benefits, and asking what the activity is *for* misconstrues the motivation [Woolley & Fishbach, Means-Ends Fusion chapter, Goal Systems Theory, OUP, 2023 (chapter dated forthcoming in ms.)]. This is the corpus's strongest internal challenge to default why-laddering: not every stated goal conceals a deeper one.

**Self-concordance** shows that the gap between stated and underlying goals has measurable consequences. Goals consistent with a person's developing interests and core values receive more sustained effort, are more often attained, and yield greater well-being on attainment, with effects mediated by need satisfaction and shown independent of self-efficacy, implementation intentions, avoidance framing, and life skills across three longitudinal samples [Sheldon & Elliot, JPSP, 1999]. The paper's opening motivating observation states the phenomenon in one sentence: individuals can attain their goals "but to be no happier than before" [Sheldon & Elliot, JPSP, 1999]; the authors offer this as motivation for the research, not as a measured prevalence. **Miswanting** extends the problem past elicitation altogether: people systematically mispredict their future affective reactions, so even a deep goal accurately recovered by skilled questioning may be miswanted [Gilbert & Wilson, "Miswanting," in Forgas (ed.), Thinking and Feeling, Cambridge UP, 2000]. No corpus source reconciles the tension between "deep goals are recoverable by reflection" and "recovered deep goals can still be wrong about future feeling."

## 1.2 Goal misattribution and the limits of introspection

The corpus does not contain the classic introspection-limits literature directly (the Nisbett–Wilson "telling more than we can know" lineage appears only as passing citations to Nisbett & Ross, 1980, inside other sources [Rivard, FIU dissertation, 2014; Wilson & Gilbert, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 2003]). What it does contain are three operational demonstrations that reported goal-reasons are unreliable and manipulable:

1. **People choose more self-concordant goals when prompted to examine motives first.** Participants who rated candidate goals for self-concordance *before* selecting them chose more intrinsic, value-matched goals than those not given the opportunity, with the effect mediated by the concordance ratings themselves; intrinsic selection in turn predicted increased well-being [Sheldon, Prentice & Osin, Journal of Research in Personality, 2019]. The implication cuts both ways: unprompted goal statements are demonstrably improvable, meaning the default statement misrepresents what the person would endorse on reflection.

2. **Why-laddering can manufacture rather than recover structure.** The means-end chain method, the canonical attribute→consequence→value probe behind "why is that important to you?" [Reynolds & Gutman, Journal of Advertising Research, 1988, via corpus laddering texts], lacks "a complete and formalized means-end theory that supports a single methodology," with unresolved methodological debates over attribute elicitation, hard vs. soft laddering, cut-off levels, and interpretation [Kilwinger & van Dam, Psychology & Marketing, 2021]. Whether repeated why-probes yield stable value hierarchies or forced-abstraction artifacts is an open question the corpus poses but does not settle (a round-2.5 deepening pass retrieved test-retest and convergent-validity source candidates but no extracted findings).

3. **The interviewer's prior contaminates what is elicited.** In a randomized forensic paradigm (584 participants randomly assigned to interviewer or witness roles within pairs), interviewers with *no* pre-interview knowledge elicited more information from witnesses at equal accuracy than informed interviewers, and explicit instructions to avoid suggestive questions did not remove the informed-interviewer penalty; the mechanism review traces this to confirmation bias and interpersonal expectancy effects: interviewers holding a hypothesis select confirming questions and elicit hypothesis-consistent answers regardless of incentive to be accurate [Rivard, FIU dissertation, 2014, reviewing Snyder & Swann, 1978, and the Rosenthal expectancy tradition; fuller treatment in Section 5]. For goal elicitation the transfer is direct: an elicitor who already "knows" what the user really wants will find it, whether or not it is there. [confidence: transfer from forensic to goal-elicitation contexts is inferential; no corpus source tests it directly]

Conversation-analytic work adds that even apparently innocent open questions embed presuppositions: "what do you expect from therapy?" assumes a goal pre-exists, is articulable, and is singular, assumptions patients observably resist [Schoeb, Studies in Communication Sciences, 2014].

## 1.3 Means-ends confusion and the XY problem

The engineering literature independently converged on the same phenomenon and gave it a name. The XY problem is "asking about your attempted solution rather than your actual problem": the user wants X, believes Y is a route to X, gets stuck on Y, and asks about Y. Helpers are confused "because Y seems like a strange problem to want to solve," and "after much interaction and wasted time, it finally becomes clear that the user really wants help with X, and that Y wasn't even a suitable solution for X" [Meta Stack Exchange, "What is the XY problem?", 2010; 2,044 score, ~849,000 views as of the retrieved page snapshot; xyproblem.info, n.d.]. The canonical worked example is the filename-extension dialogue: the asker requests the last three characters of a filename when the underlying need is the file extension, which three characters do not reliably capture [xyproblem.info, n.d.].

**Markers.** Practitioners converge on recognizable surface features: questions opening "How can I…" are treated as "almost like a red flag" provoking the counter-question "What's your use-case?" [Dodds, tkdodo.eu, 2021]; questions consisting mostly of what was tried rather than why the task exists in the first place [expo/fyi, "Don't ask about solutions," n.d.]; and stuck-on-one-approach behavior that one Stack Exchange answer explicitly links to the Einstellung effect, where a first idea blocks perception of superior alternatives [Benn answer, Meta Stack Exchange, 2010 thread]. The standard prescriptions are symmetrical: askers should volunteer the broader picture and explain why ruled-out alternatives were ruled out [xyproblem.info; Meta Stack Exchange, 2010]; helpers should ask "why", with one practitioner's caution that why-questions can be heard as rebukes ("Why did you install a second Python?" read as "you shouldn't have"), so phrasing should mark the question as information-seeking [Batchelder, nedbatchelder.com, 2013]; this is a practitioner observation, not reception-frequency evidence.

**Prevalence.** FLAG: the corpus contains *no measured prevalence* of the XY problem in any help-seeking corpus. The only quantitative-sounding claim is Batchelder's report that knowing the underlying reason changes his answer "75% of the time": an explicit practitioner self-estimate, not a measured rate [Batchelder, 2013]. Question-quality research on Stack Overflow establishes that answer quality is strongly positively correlated with question quality [Yao, Tong, Xie, Akoglu, Xu & Lü, arXiv 1311.6876, 2013], which is consistent with, but does not measure, an XY mechanism. A round-2.5 retrieval pass aimed at prevalence returned post-classification studies but no extracted XY-rate finding. Any number attached to "how common is the XY problem" is currently unsupported.

**Causes as practitioners see them.** The folklore offers multiple, not mutually exclusive, mechanisms: getting "stuck on what they believe is the solution" and unable to step back [xyproblem.info]; working forward from problem to solution and asking about the point of sticking [Batchelder, 2013]; deliberately asking a narrow question "to not take up too much of the helper's time" [Batchelder, 2013]; and, inside organizations, junior engineers assuming all solutions were already chosen for them ("solution blinders"), so that redirecting from Y to X is framed as a core senior-engineer teaching function [cjc25.com, "The XY-problem and senior engineering," 2022]. These map respectively onto the cognitive (Einstellung/action-identification), structural (the attempted solution is what is perceptually present), and social mechanisms. The corpus names all three but contains no study adjudicating which dominates where.

**Requirements-elicitation parallels.** Requirements engineering is the professionalized response to the same gap: elicitation is explicitly "not merely a matter of transcribing exactly what users say" but a search-and-consolidation activity [Palomares, Franch, Quer, Chatzipetrou, López & Gorschek, Requirements Engineering Journal, 2020/2021]; stakeholder statements are analyzed by their "grounds", direct past experience versus hypothetical reasoning, a formal analogue of the surface/deep distinction [Burnay, Bouraga, Gillain & Jureta, Software Quality Journal, 2020]; and controlled experiments establish that elicitation technique measurably changes what is elicited [Rueda, Panach & Distante, Information and Software Technology, 2020], the engineering counterpart to the psychology finding that goal statements depend on how they are asked for. (Full treatment of this literature in Section 3.)

## 1.4 Motivated reasoning around goals

The corpus's coverage of *motivated* misstatement, where the gap between stated and real goals serves a purpose for the speaker, is thinner than its coverage of cognitive mechanisms, and should be read as partially evidenced.

What is supported: self-concordance theory distinguishes goals pursued for autonomous reasons from goals pursued for introjected (guilt/approval) or external reasons, and shows that people demonstrably pursue goals that do not express their enduring interests and values [Sheldon & Elliot, JPSP, 1999]. That is, some stated goals are adopted to satisfy others rather than the self, and the speaker may not distinguish the two without prompting [Sheldon, Prentice & Osin, 2019]. On the disclosure side, question phrasing interacts with the answerer's motives: negative-assumption questions elicit more truthful disclosure than positive-assumption or general questions [Minson, VanEpps, Yip & Schweitzer, OBHDP, 2018]: direct evidence that people shade what they reveal, and that the elicitor's phrasing partly controls the shading. The help-seeking folklore adds the face-saving motive: askers narrow their question to appear competent or to economize the helper's attention [Batchelder, 2013; cjc25.com, 2022 notes the XY dynamic "can make the question asker feel dumb"].

FLAG, corpus gap: a round-2.5 pass surfaced titles from a support-seeking literature on impression-management concerns and stigma in choosing what to disclose when asking for help (e.g., a "stigma support activation model" and studies of impression goals across communication channels), but no full texts were retrieved into the corpus, so no specific finding from that literature can be reported here. The claim that stigma and impression management systematically distort *stated goals* in help-seeking is plausible and pointed to by retrieved titles, but is unverified in this corpus.

# Dialogic Methods: The Therapeutic Lineage

<!-- editorial:background -->
The methods in the therapeutic and coaching tradition treat a person's initially stated goal as raw material rather than final answer. Motivational interviewing reads it as a pole of an unresolved ambivalence; solution-focused brief therapy as a problem-description not yet converted into a picture of a preferred future; ACT values clarification as a possible proxy for, or betrayal of, an underlying valued direction; Socratic questioning in CBT as the output of unexamined appraisals; immunity-to-change mapping as the visible half of a commitment structure whose hidden half works against it. They share, as a family resemblance rather than a uniform rule, an evocative stance: the client's own language and reasons are treated as the engine, and the practitioner elicits rather than installs. The resemblance is loose, though: solution-focused questions are built as interventions that carry purposeful presuppositions, Socratic questioning directs attention toward selected evidence, and immunity-to-change mapping imposes its own interpretive structure on what the client reports. They differ sharply in how much steering they permit and how much empirical scrutiny each has survived.
<!-- /editorial -->

## Motivational interviewing: a comparatively well-tested method in this corpus, and the test is partly disconfirming

Motivational interviewing (MI) is defined by its founders as "a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation and commitment to change" [Miller & Rollnick 2013, quoted in Magill et al., Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 2014]. Its "spirit" has three named components: collaboration (a partnership acknowledging the patient's expertise about themselves), evocation (activating the person's own reasons for change), and honoring autonomy (the explicit "it's up to you") [Lubman, Hall & Gibbie, Australian Family Physician, 2012, presenting Miller & Rollnick's framework]. The training literature warns that MI strategies used without the spirit are ineffective, a claim the founders assert rather than demonstrate [Lubman et al., 2012]. The basic skills are the OARS quartet: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries [Lubman et al., 2012]. The guiding principles are compressed into RULE, and the first letter is the theory of why stated goals mislead: Resist the righting reflex (elsewhere in this report also called the advice-giving reflex; the terms are synonyms) [Lubman et al., 2012].

The righting reflex is the helper's impulse to fix, advise, and advocate for the "good" side of the person's dilemma. MI's core theoretical claim is that an ambivalent person holds both sides at once, so when the helper argues for change, the client is left to voice the other side, and "as a person defends the status quo, the likelihood of change decreases" [SAMHSA TIP 35, 2019; also UNODC Treatnet training materials]. On this account the stated goal ("I want to quit") misleads not because it is false but because it is half of the truth; the counter-commitment is present but unstated, and MI's training theory proposes that directive help may surface it as opposition. The supporting process evidence for this mechanism is correlational, not a randomized manipulation of helper behaviour (Section 5).

MI names four sequential processes: engaging, focusing, evoking, planning [Miller & Rollnick, via Gobat et al., European Journal for Person Centered Healthcare, 2018]. The focusing process is MI's explicit machinery for goal selection: identifying candidate topics, prioritising one from many (the "agenda mapping" strategy), then holding the conversation on the agreed focus so change talk in that area can be recognised and reinforced [Gobat et al., 2018]. Gobat and colleagues also note that institutional agendas often only partially map onto the change conversation the client is ready to have, so the focus itself must be negotiated, not assumed [Gobat et al., 2018]. The signature question moves are open questions that evoke the client's own reasons ("What concerns do you have about your drinking?" rather than "Are you concerned about your drinking?"), reflections that differentially strengthen change talk, and explicit linkage of the behaviour to the client's values and future goals [Lubman et al., 2012].

The empirical standing is unusually well specified, because MI's causal model (therapist skill → client change talk → outcome) has been meta-analytically tested twice [Magill et al., JCCP, 2014; Magill et al., 2018, PubMed 29265832]. The headline pattern: MI-consistent skill evokes more client change talk, but change talk fails to predict outcome as an independent effect, while sustain talk (client language against change) predicts worse outcomes; MI-inconsistent behaviour (confronting, warning, unsolicited direction) is the most consistent negative predictor, and the relational hypothesis, that empathy and MI spirit directly cause outcomes, was unsupported in trial data [Magill et al., 2014; Magill et al., 2018; Apodaca & Longabaugh, Addiction, 2009]. (Full meta-analytic figures in Section 5.)

Outcome-level evidence is more modest than the method's reputation: small durable effects against weak comparators, non-significant differences against specific alternative treatments, and, awkwardly, larger effects when MI practice was not manual-guided [Lundahl et al., Research on Social Work Practice, 2010; Hettema, Steele & Miller, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology, 2005]. (Figures in Section 5.) The composite reading, consistent across these sources: MI's most replicated process signal is subtractive, and it is correlational. What is most consistently associated with better outcomes is the absence of MI-inconsistent behaviour and a low proportion of sustain talk, not the accumulation of evocative technique.

## Solution-focused brief therapy: questions as interventions

Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT), developed at the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee by de Shazer, Berg and colleagues, co-constructs solutions by focusing on desired futures, strengths, and past successes instead of analysing problems and their causes [Neipp, Beyebach, Sanchez-Prada & Delgado Álvarez, Journal of Family Therapy, 2021; Franklin et al., Research on Social Work Practice, 2024]. Its theory of why stated goals mislead is linguistic and constructivist: solution-focused questions "are not understood as mere information-gathering tools, but as interventions in their own right," carrying therapeutic force through the presuppositions they convey [McGee et al. 2005, as presented in Neipp et al., 2021]. A goal stated inside problem-talk inherits the problem frame; the method's questions are designed to move the client into a frame where a workable goal can be described at all.

The three signature moves [Neipp et al., 2021; Lee, Eads & Magier, Social Workers' Desk Reference chapter, 2022]:

- The **miracle question** invites the client to describe in detail their preferred future, as if the problem had disappeared overnight, and was developed explicitly as a way to prompt detailed description of goals [de Shazer 1991, via Neipp et al., 2021].
- **Exception questions** locate occasions when the problem could have happened but did not, to surface solutions the client is already using [de Shazer et al. 1986, via Neipp et al., 2021].
- **Scaling questions** concretise: rating progress 0 to 10 and describing what "one point more" would look like invites small next steps [de Shazer 1994, via Neipp et al., 2021].

The head-to-head evidence is the corpus's most direct test of whether specific question types have specific effects. In a randomized study, 246 participants described a personal problem and answered problem-focused, miracle, exception, or scaling questions. The miracle and exception conditions reduced negative affect more than the problem-focused condition; the scaling condition generated more action steps; but the authors' summary judgment was that "solution-focused and problem-focused questions might be more similar than different in their immediate impact on clients" [Neipp et al., Journal of Family Therapy, 2021, "Solution-focused versus problem-focused questions"]. The advertised specificity is real but modest: future/exception questions move feeling, scaling moves concreteness. Sample caveat: psychology undergraduates answering written questions about a self-chosen problem, not a clinical population.

Outcome evidence spans a wide range. The early meta-analysis found small effects (d = 0.13 to 0.26) with only internalizing problems reaching significance [Kim, Research on Social Work Practice, 2008]. The recent meta-analysis of randomized trials in community-based services found a medium pooled effect (g = 0.654, 95% CI 0.386–0.922, 28 studies, 340 effect sizes), with interventions using four or more SFBT techniques across the categories of cooperative language and co-construction, strengths and resources, and future-focused questions showing a moderate effect [Franklin, Ding, Kim, Zhang et al., Research on Social Work Practice, 2024, "Solution-Focused Brief Therapy in Community-Based Services"; figures verified against the retrieved abstract]. [disputed: pooled SFBT effect sizes range from d ≈ 0.13–0.26 (Kim 2008, all settings, mixed designs) to g ≈ 0.65 (Franklin et al. 2024, community-based RCTs only); the populations, decades, and inclusion rules differ, so the figures should not be averaged.]

## ACT values clarification: separating directions from destinations

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy's values work rests on a categorical distinction: goals are completable achievements, while values are ongoing chosen directions, "a direction that you would like to move toward," never finished [VA Whole Health Library, "Values," n.d., presenting Kelly Wilson's framework]. Wilson's technical definition of values is "freely chosen, verbally constructed consequences of dynamic, evolving patterns of activity for which the predominant reinforcer becomes intrinsic to the behavioral pattern itself" [Wilson, via VA Whole Health Library]. The theory of why stated goals mislead follows directly: a stated goal may be a socially compliant or avoidance-driven proxy that has drifted from any valued direction, and cognitive fusion (treating one's thoughts and self-stories as literal reality) keeps the drift invisible. Defusion techniques, which train the person to observe thoughts as thoughts, are the companion move that loosens the stated goal's grip so the underlying direction can be examined. Measurement exists: the Valued Living Questionnaire has clients rate the importance of ten life domains and the consistency of their recent actions with each, making the value-action gap explicit [Wilson, Sandoz, Kitchens & Roberts, The Psychological Record manuscript]. Signature exercises are experiential rather than interrogative: domain ratings, metaphor work (values as compass headings), and structured writing about what the person wants their life to be for [VA Whole Health Library].

The empirical standing is moderate and mechanism-friendly. A meta-analysis of 66 laboratory-based component studies found significant positive effects for values components against inactive comparisons, with a design lesson attached: effect sizes were larger for components delivered through experiential methods (metaphors, exercises) than through rationale alone [Levin et al., Behavior Therapy, 2012]. A randomized multiple-baseline study of 15 treatment-seeking adults compared a values/committed-action module (ACT ENGAGED) with an acceptance/defusion module (ACT OPEN): both improved symptoms and quality of life, but ENGAGED produced greater gains in quality of life and values-based activation while OPEN produced greater gains in symptom severity, acceptance, and defusion, evidence that the values component has specific, separable effects [Villatte et al., Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2016, PubMed 26716932]. Adding a brief personal-values component to an acceptance intervention increased pain tolerance beyond acceptance alone in an experimental pain task [Branstetter-Rost, Cushing & Douleh, Journal of Pain, 2009]. A systematic review of values interventions reports consistently positive but methodologically heterogeneous results [Rahal & Gon, International Journal of Psychology & Psychological Therapy, 2020], and observational treatment data link improvements in depression to changes in both defusion and values-based action [Improvements in Depression... study, PMC5830477, n.d.]. FLAG: most component studies are laboratory analogues with convenience samples; the clinical dismantling base is one 15-person single-case-design study.

## Socratic questioning in CBT: guided discovery with a measured mechanism

Socratic questioning is the maieutic core of cognitive therapy: drawing out ideas rather than implanting them, first inviting the person to question held beliefs and notice contradictions, then to construct alternatives [Carona, BJPsych Advances, 2020/2021]. The canonical craft criteria come from Padesky: good Socratic questions are ones "the client has the knowledge to answer," that draw attention to relevant information outside the client's current focus, that move from concrete to abstract, and that let the client re-evaluate a prior conclusion or build a new one [Padesky 1993, quoted in Carona, 2020/2021]. The stated aim is guided discovery, not persuasion: not changing the patient's mind but guiding them to discover methods for improving their lives [Carona, 2020/2021]. The implied theory of misleading goals: what a person says they want rests on appraisals and inferences they have not examined, and the durable correction is one they derive themselves.

The mechanism evidence is small but unusually clean for a single technique. In 55 depressed adults in a 16-week course of cognitive therapy, observer-rated within-patient increases in therapist Socratic questioning in the first three sessions predicted next-session symptom improvement: a one-standard-deviation increase predicted a 1.51-point BDI-II decrease the following session, and the effect survived controlling for within-patient therapeutic alliance ratings, the first empirical support of its kind [Braun, Strunk, Sasso & Cooper, Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2015]. A larger sample (123 CBT clients) then tested the pathway: cognitive change significantly mediated the relation between Socratic questioning and symptom change, and the effect of questioning on cognitive change was stronger for clients who entered treatment with weaker CBT skills [Vittorio, Murphy, Braun & Strunk, Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2022]. Moderator work on the original sample found the questioning-improvement link stronger among clients with greater pessimistic bias about future life events and among those not on antidepressant medication, with small effects [Vittorio, Braun, Cheavens & Strunk, Cognitive Therapy and Research, 2021]. Honest limits: this is one research group, observational process data from trials rather than experimental manipulation of questioning, and depression-specific; it establishes a plausible mechanism, not a portable causal law.

## Immunity to change: mapping the goal's opposition

Kegan and Lahey's immunity-to-change (ITC) method begins from a diagnosis of why sincere goals stall: "even as they hold a sincere commitment to change, many people unwittingly apply productive energy toward a hidden competing commitment," producing an internal conflict that looks like resistance but is "a kind of personal immunity to change" [Kegan & Lahey, Harvard Business Review, 2001]. The stated goal misleads not because it is insincere but because it is one foot on the gas while an unnamed commitment keeps the other foot on the brake; the failure to enact the visible commitment is the success of an invisible one [Kegan & Lahey, via Minds at Work practitioner materials, n.d.].

The signature instrument is the four-column map: (1) the improvement goal, something genuinely wanted and repeatedly unachieved; (2) an honest inventory of the behaviours that work against it; (3) the hidden competing commitments those behaviours serve; (4) the "big assumptions," deeply held beliefs that make the competing commitments feel necessary, held not as beliefs but as the lens through which the world is seen [Kegan & Lahey, HBR, 2001; Minds at Work, n.d.]. Change then proceeds by designing small tests of the big assumption rather than by willpower [Minds at Work, n.d.]. The question moves are diagnostic prompts down the columns: what are you doing or not doing that undermines the goal; what would you be afraid of if you did the opposite; what commitment does that fear reveal; what must you be assuming for that commitment to feel necessary.

The evidence base is thin, and should be stated as such. The first and, in this corpus, only formal quantitative efficacy study compared 45 supervisors who received ITC coaching with 25 comparison supervisors from the same company: the coached group reported significantly more progress on leadership development goals, and participants rated the four-column map the most useful component [Markus, Journal of Applied Behavioral Science, 2013]. FLAG: single study, self-reported goal progress, non-randomized comparison group, low citation uptake; directionally positive, not confirmatory. The remaining literature is qualitative and interpretive [Reams, Integral Review, 2016; Reams & Reams, Oxford Brookes coaching research], plus a registered clinical application to medication adherence [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02846220] whose corpus record contains no outcome results. Practitioner materials repeat a striking statistic about cardiac patients failing to change life-saving habits; the figure circulates through the method's own marketing channel without a primary citation in this corpus and is omitted here.

## What the five methods jointly establish

The five lineages converge on three empirically anchored points. (a) Directive fixing is the most consistent negative process correlate; MI-inconsistent behaviour is the most consistent negative predictor across observational process studies [Magill et al., JCCP, 2014; Magill et al., 2018, PubMed 29265832; Apodaca & Longabaugh, Addiction, 2009]. (b) Specific question types have real but modest specific effects: future and exception framing shifts affect, scaling produces action steps [Neipp et al., Journal of Family Therapy, 2021], and experiential engagement outperforms rationale-giving [Levin et al., Behavior Therapy, 2012]. (c) Client-generated cognition is associated with change in the cited process analyses: cognitive change mediated the link between Socratic questioning and symptom change in observational trial data [Vittorio et al., Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2022], and MI outcomes track the client's own language balance rather than the therapist's technique count [Magill et al., 2018, PubMed 29265832]. They diverge on where the "real" goal lives: MI and ITC treat it as recoverable by working through ambivalence and hidden commitments; SFBT declines to excavate and builds the goal forward from a described future; ACT relocates the question from goals to directions. No study in this corpus compares these excavation-versus-construction strategies head to head.

# Dialogic Methods: the Coaching, Design-Research, and Engineering Lineage

Outside the clinic, a second family of questioning methods grew up in coaching rooms, market-research interviews, factory floors, and software teams. All share the therapeutic literature's core suspicion of the first stated goal, treating it as potentially incomplete or misframed rather than as the operative one, but each locates the failure differently: in a missing conversational structure (GROW), in the questioner's contaminating language (Clean Language), in the respondent's inability to articulate the value behind a preference (laddering), in mistaking the product for the progress it serves (Jobs-to-be-Done), in stopping at symptoms (Five Whys), or in stakeholders stating solutions instead of needs (requirements engineering, where the XY problem is the folk name for the same pathology). For each: what it is, why its designers think stated goals mislead, its signature question moves, and the state of the evidence.

<!-- editorial:background -->
A partial genealogy runs under these methods. The coaching, Clean Language, and laddering lineages descend from humanistic and constructivist practice: the interviewee is treated as the expert on their own experience, the questioner as a facilitator whose main risk is contaminating the account with their own frames. The factory and engineering traditions arrived at a similar suspicion of stated goals independently, out of defect analysis rather than out of a theory of the person. The methods diverge on structure. The coaching lineage imposes a staged conversational sequence and trusts the client to fill it. The design-research lineage imposes an analytical target (a value hierarchy, a job map) and engineers questions backward from it. The engineering lineage treats elicitation as an error-prone measurement process and asks which instruments yield the fewest defects downstream.

## GROW: structure without dismantling evidence

GROW (Goal, Reality, Options, Will) is a widely taught coaching sequence, described in its own practitioner literature as "the default, go to model for most coaches" [Panchal & Riddell, "The GROWS model: extending the GROW coaching model to support behavioural change", The Coaching Psychologist, 2020]; that characterization is the practitioners' own, not a finding from comparative uptake data. Its implicit theory of misleading goals is structural rather than psychodynamic: people fail to change not because their stated goal is false but because change conversations wander, skipping either the honest audit of current reality or the commitment step. The signature moves are staged open questions: goal ("what do you want?"), reality ("what is happening now?"), option-generation, and will/commitment questions, applied flexibly rather than strictly in sequence [Panchal & Riddell, 2020].

The evidence picture has a specific shape: coaching in aggregate works, but nothing isolates GROW as the reason. Meta-analytic reviews find workplace coaching effective on organizational outcomes overall, while conceding the field cannot yet say "what works, what the underlying mechanisms of action are, which coaching approaches are most effective" [Cannon-Bowers et al., Frontiers in Psychology, 2023; Grover & Furnham, PLOS ONE, 2016]. The 2023 meta-analysis notes the root problem: coaching is defined so loosely (counseling, facilitation, goal setting, appreciative inquiry) that comparing studies is difficult. The corpus contains no dismantling study testing GROW against another structure or against unstructured conversation. What exists instead is: (a) a randomized controlled trial (N = 202 clients, 13 organizations) finding coaching effective regardless of clients' goal-orientation disposition, with a twist: framing the goal as a learning goal predicted higher perceived achievement inside coaching but *lower* perceived achievement without a coach ["Do I need coaching to achieve a learning goal? Coaching, goal orientation, and goal framing: a randomized control trial study", Current Psychology, 2025; author byline not captured in the corpus extract]; (b) a controlled field experiment (N = 84) finding coaching benefits concentrated in coachees high in openness and avoid-goal orientation and low in core self-evaluations [Jones, Woods & Zhou, "The Effects of Coachee Personality and Goal Orientation on Performance Improvement Following Coaching: A Controlled Field Experiment", 2019]; and (c) GROW's own extension literature, candid that the model under-supports the action phase. The GROWS proposal grafts obstacle-anticipation and relapse-recovery questions from the Health Action Process Approach onto GROW's Will stage; its supporting study is a pilot with four coaching clients [Panchal & Riddell, 2020]. FLAG: any efficacy claim for GROWS rests on those four clients and should not be quoted as evidence.

Two adjacent findings cut against GROW's goal-first orthodoxy. Transcript analysis of experienced therapists found few behaviors aimed at explicit early goal agreement; experts worked instead on meaningfulness and ambivalence [Oddli & McLeod, European Journal of Psychotherapy & Counselling, 2014], and a heterodox coaching strand argues for subtle goal exploration over premature goal setting [Jinks & Dexter, International Journal of Evidence Based Coaching and Mentoring, 2012]. And a GROW-based chatbot matched human coaches on goal attainment over ten months in paired RCTs, which the authors read as a warning that simple model-based coaching is mechanizable, not as a triumph for the model [Terblanche, Molyn, de Haan & Nilsson, PLOS ONE, 2022].

## Clean Language and Symbolic Modelling: the presupposition-minimal extreme

Clean Language originates with the therapist David Grove, who developed it in the 1980s while working with trauma clients' metaphors [Grove & Panzer, 1989, via Small & Ey; cleanlanguage.com history pages, n.d.]. Its theory of misleading goals is linguistic: every ordinary question smuggles in the asker's assumptions, metaphors, and preferred frames, so what the interviewee reports is partly an artifact of how they were asked. "Clean" is defined against "the unintended influence of the questioner," not against dirt [Small & Ey, Naturalistic Decision Making conference, 2024]. The premise has independent experimental support from another literature entirely: presupposition-laden questions measurably distort what people later report [Loftus, Cognitive Psychology, 1975], though Clean Language's own validity claims have not been tested in that paradigm.

The signature moves are a small fixed set of questions built exclusively from the interviewee's own words and metaphors ("and what kind of X is that X?", "and is there anything else about X?", "and then what happens?"), governed by three rules: use only the interviewee's literal verbal and non-verbal expressions; minimize added content and assumptions; help the interviewee elaborate [Nehyba & Svojanovsky, via Small & Ey, 2024]. Symbolic Modelling, developed by Lawley and Tompkins from Grove's practice, extends this into a full method for mapping a client's metaphor landscape [Lawley & Tompkins, Symbolic Modelling chapter, 2011, hosted at cleanlanguage.com]. A useful export is the "cleanness continuum," Lawley's four-class scoring of questions from classically clean to strongly leading, proposed as a coding instrument for interview quality [Lawley, 2010; Small & Ey, 2024].

Empirical standing: this is the weakest evidence base in the section, and the provenance is concentrated. The strongest peer-reviewed anchor is a qualitative demonstration study in which Clean Language interviews elicited the naturally occurring work-life-balance metaphors of six mid-career managers, arguing the method enhances rigour by keeping researchers' extraneous metaphors out of the account [Tosey, Lawley & Meese, British Journal of Management, 2014]. Note the authorship: Lawley is a practitioner-principal of the method, and the study was a partnership with the Clean Change Company. Beyond that, the evidence is coachee-experience studies [Linder-Pelz & Lawley, International Coaching Psychology Review, 2015], applied case reports (midwifery decision-making, novice teachers), and the explicitly exploratory cognitive-task-analysis proposal [Small & Ey, 2024]. FLAG: most Clean Language sources in the corpus are hosted by the practitioner community itself (cleanlanguage.com), including preprints of the peer-reviewed papers; claims of superior elicitation validity are practitioner-attributed, with no independent replication, no controlled comparison against ordinary open questioning, and no outcome trials.

## Laddering and means-end chain interviewing: a formalized "why" with known artifacts

Laddering is the canonical formalization of repeated why-probing. Rooted in means-end chain theory [Gutman, 1982, via Kilwinger & van Dam, 2021] and personal construct theory [Kelly, 1955, via Kilwinger & van Dam], it was codified by Reynolds and Gutman: elicit the attributes a respondent uses to distinguish products, then climb via the directed probe "why is that important to you?" through consequences to terminal personal values, producing attribute-consequence-value (A-C-V) chains that are content-coded, aggregated in an implication matrix, and drawn as a hierarchical value map [Reynolds & Gutman, Journal of Advertising Research, 1988]. Its theory of misleading goals is that stated preferences sit at the bottom of a hierarchy the respondent has never examined; only probing reveals which values the consequences serve [Reynolds & Gutman, 1988]. The manual is explicit about the interpersonal engineering required: position the respondent as the expert, and even create "a slight sense of vulnerability on the part of the interviewer" to license introspection [Reynolds & Gutman, 1988].

The method has two variants. Soft laddering is the original conversational interview; hard laddering forces respondents up the ladder via structured questionnaires or software. A review by the method's co-originator argues that several hard-laddering implementations violate the technique's fundamental assumptions, and that discrepant published conclusions about whether means-end hierarchies are even hierarchical are attributable to those violations [Phillips & Reynolds, Qualitative Market Research, 2009].

Reliability is the sore point, and the corpus's best methodological source is frank about it. Kilwinger and van Dam report that despite widespread acceptance there is no standardized way to analyze laddering data and "the effects of different analyses on the results are largely unknown": a construct a respondent never mentions can still be important to them; coding versus condensing constructs at different abstraction levels changes results in both directions; available software packages do not even agree on the aggregation algorithm; and the method's assumptions fail silently when exported to new domains [Kilwinger & van Dam, "Methodological considerations on the means-end chain analysis revisited", Psychology & Marketing, 2021]. The same tradition documents interviewer effects and forced-abstraction artifacts: respondents pushed up the ladder can manufacture value talk to satisfy the probe [Kilwinger & van Dam, 2021; Grunert & Grunert, 1995, as cited therein]. A targeted round-2.5 retrieval on test-retest stability of laddered value hierarchies returned validation sources (LadderUX, between-method convergent-validity comparisons, a "snakes versus ladders" validation study) but no read full text establishing a stability coefficient; the corpus documents the artifacts in detail and the stability question remains open [round2_5 answer on laddering reliability, source list only]. For a system that wants to run "why is that important to you?" loops on users, the transferable finding is that the ladder is partly a product of the probing, coding, and aggregation choices, not a pre-existing structure read off the mind.

## Jobs-to-be-Done: causal reframing from product to progress

JTBD's theory of misleading goals is a category claim, and it is the framework's own claim rather than a demonstrated finding: people describe wants in terms of products and features, but the unit that actually drives demand, JTBD asserts, is the "job," the progress a person is trying to make in a circumstance. The lineage is contested, which matters for evidence weighting: Christensen popularized Jobs Theory at Harvard; Ulwick presented his Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) process to Christensen in 1999 and claims priority for the practical method; Moesta developed the interview-craft wing into Demand-Side Sales [GoPractice, 2024; anthonyulwick.com, 2016; jobs-to-be-done.com history, 2017].

The signature question moves differ by wing. The Christensen/Moesta wing centers the *switch interview*: reconstruct, almost forensically, the timeline of a real past purchase or switch, and map the four forces operating on it: the push of frustration with the current solution, the pull of the new one, the anxiety of the new, and the habit or comfort of the incumbent. Christensen's own illustration is his years-long resistance to replacing his mobile phone because anxiety and habit outweighed push and pull [Christensen, Competing Against Luck, as summarized in GoPractice, 2024]. The Ulwick/ODI wing replaces narrative with structure: map the job into process steps, run one or two ethnographic interviews for context, then elicit for each step the *metrics customers use to measure success* (desired outcome statements), stopping only "when all attempts to capture outcomes related to speed, predictability and output have been exhausted for each process step" [Ulwick, marketingjournal.org, 2016]. Ulwick's explicit anti-move parallels the XY prescription: never ask "how would you improve this product?"; ask instead what makes the job inconvenient, time-consuming, unstable, or wasteful [Ulwick, 2016]. Practitioners insist the interviews are only step one of a quantitative pipeline: the purpose is patterns across many respondents, "not prioritized pain points" from a handful of chats [Boysen, jtbd.one, 2025; Boysen, jobs-to-be-done.com, 2018].

Empirical standing: almost entirely practitioner-authored and originator-hosted (Strategyn, jobs-to-be-done.com, Substack). The corpus contains well-specified procedure and rich case narrative but no independent controlled comparison of JTBD interviewing against alternative elicitation methods, and no peer-reviewed outcome evidence. FLAG: success-rate claims that circulate around ODI (e.g., project hit-rate figures on Strategyn's site) are vendor self-reports and are not carried into this report. Notably, a prominent JTBD practitioner now argues generative AI can replace much of the interview corpus itself, retaining human interviews only for segment deep-dives [Boysen, jtbd.one, 2025], a position statement rather than a tested result.

## Five Whys: the cautionary tale

Five Whys is root-cause questioning at its most compressed: ask "why?" repeatedly, canonically five times, until a root cause "becomes clear." It originates in the Toyota Production System; Taiichi Ohno called repeated why-asking "the basis of Toyota's scientific approach" and coined "five whys equal one how" [Ohno, as quoted in Card, BMJ Quality & Safety, 2017]. Its implicit theory is temporal: the first answer describes a symptom, and only iteration reaches the cause worth fixing.

The serious literature in the corpus is uniformly critical, and no source defends the method empirically. Card's analysis is the anchor: Five Whys' popularity in healthcare root-cause analysis "is not the result of any evidence that it is effective" but owes to "pedigree, simplicity and pedagogy"; the method forces users down a single analytical pathway, insists on a single root cause, and assumes the most distal link is the best intervention point, when equally valid alternative why-chains from the same incident lead to entirely different causes and solutions [Card, BMJ Quality & Safety, 2017]. Even the method's favorite teaching case collapses on inspection: the Washington Monument lighting story is really the Lincoln Memorial, the causal details are wrong, and the celebrated solution (delaying the lights) was reversed after tourist complaints, a failure of systems breadth the five-why chain never surfaced [Card, 2017, citing Gross]. Toyota's own successors flag the reliance on off-the-cuff deduction over situated observation [Minoura, via Card, 2017]. A survey-based study quantifies the drift from method to ritual: over half of the reviewed literature presents Five Whys as a simplified brainstorming tool, 30.0% of authors are unclear on intended use, only 16.7% clearly indicate that each "why" requires actual investigation and empirical methods, and 41.8% of surveyed respondents use it as a purely brainstorming tool ["Five whys: a possible path to failure", International Journal of Six Sigma and Competitive Advantage, 15(1), 2024; authors not captured in the corpus record]. FLAG: the only quantitative effectiveness review in the corpus is a master's thesis on facilitation factors [Key, WKU thesis, 2019], student work, not peer-reviewed evidence. The lesson for elicitation design is precise: iterated "why" chains inherit all of the asker's blind spots, linearize multi-cause situations, and feel rigorous while doing so.

## Requirements elicitation: the professionalized XY problem

Software engineering treats the gap between stated and actual need as an industrial defect class. The folk version is the XY problem (treated in full in Section 1.3): the help-seeker asks about their attempted solution Y instead of the underlying problem X, and the community's standing prescription is to ask what they are really trying to do [Meta Stack Exchange, 2010; xyproblem.info, n.d.]. FLAG: the often-quoted estimate that knowing the underlying reason changes the answer "75% of the time" is one engineer's self-report, not a measured rate [Batchelder, 2013].

The research literature makes three empirical contributions the softer traditions lack. First, statement quality varies by *ground*: stakeholder statements built on direct past experience versus hypothetical reasoning have measurably different qualities, giving an interviewer an evidence-backed probe ("have you actually done/seen this, or are you inferring it?") [Burnay, Bouraga, Gillain & Jureta, Software Quality Journal, 2020]. Second, elicitation-interview methods have been compared head-to-head in a controlled family of experiments, a level of rigor no coaching or JTBD comparison in the corpus reaches [Rueda, Panach & Distante, Information and Software Technology, 2020]. Third, practice diverges from lab: a 24-practitioner study across 12 Swedish IT companies found group techniques dominate over one-on-one interviews except in small projects, with the persistent challenges being understanding and prioritizing stakeholder needs, and requirements instability [Palomares et al., Requirements Engineering Journal, 2021]. FLAG: the claim quoted in that paper that "80% of products lose money due to wrongly set product objectives" traces to an industry white paper [Stallabaum & Ly, 2016, as cited in Palomares et al., 2021], not to measured data. On stakeholder goal modelling: formal goal-oriented frameworks (KAOS, i*) appear in the corpus only as passing mentions in technique inventories [Palomares et al., 2021]; the corpus contains no primary empirical study of goal-model-driven elicitation, so no effectiveness claims are made here. The field's foundational stance is the right closing note: requirements "do not exist somewhere 'out there', merely waiting to be collected" [Nuseibeh & Easterbrook, as cited in Palomares et al., 2021]. Every method above is, in its own dialect, a response to that fact.

## What the lineage agrees on, and where the evidence actually is

The six traditions converge on three moves, with little visible cross-citation among the sources reviewed here: shift the question from the stated solution to the situation that produced it (XY prescription, switch interview, job mapping, GROW's Reality stage); control the questioner's own contamination (cleanness rules, laddering's facilitator positioning); and structure the climb from concrete to abstract rather than free-associating it (A-C-V ladders, job maps, statement grounds). The evidence gradient is equally consistent: outcome evidence exists at the level of whole interventions (coaching meta-analyses, requirements experiments), while for the named methods themselves this corpus contains no dismantling trial that isolates any of their signature question moves. The strongest empirical statements in this lineage are negative or cautionary: Five Whys' single-pathway failure [Card, 2017], laddering's analysis-dependence [Kilwinger & van Dam, 2021], and one trial family in which a scripted GROW chatbot matched the tested human coaching condition on goal attainment [Terblanche et al., 2022], a parity result for that implementation rather than proof that the method in general is mechanizable.

# 4. Problem Reframing and Goal Decomposition

## 4.1 Problem-finding versus problem-solving: the empirical base

**Sourcing caveat, stated up front. Confidence: low for every study claim in this subsection.** All of the research summarized below reaches the corpus through a single secondary source: the method author's own research summary [Wedell-Wedellsborg, "Reframing: A Research Primer", Substack, 2025]. None of the primary papers it describes is in the corpus, so none of the study designs, samples, or effect sizes has been independently verified; what follows is a report of what one practitioner-author says the literature shows, not a verified reading of that literature. The primer is candid about limitations (for example, it notes Smilansky's measure may not generalize to real-world problems), which raises its credibility as a summary, but the chain remains single-sourced and the summarizer has a commercial stake in the method.

With that boundary fixed: the primer's central claim is that identifying the right problem is a distinct, measurable capability that predicts creative performance. It describes a founding study that tracked art students on a drawing task: students who engaged in discovery-oriented, problem-exploring behavior produced drawings judged more artistic, and the primer reports that the difference tracked with career outcomes in later follow-up studies [Getzels & Csikszentmihályi, as summarized in the Wedell-Wedellsborg primer, 2025]. The primer describes later work as extending the pattern: critically acclaimed artists and scientists spending more time on problem identification than merely competent peers [Rostan, via the primer], experts in political science spending more time on the problem than novices [Voss et al., via the primer], and expert designers relying on reframing strategies [Dorst & Cross; Dorst, via the primer].

The primer reports three further findings. First, that problem-construction ability is separable from general intelligence [Smilansky; Mumford, Whetzel & Reiter-Palmon, via the primer], with the primer's own caveat that the founding measure may not generalize beyond the test instrument. Second, that it is trainable: a creativity-training meta-analysis is reported as finding training with problem-construction/reframing components related to better training outcomes and creative performance [Scott, Leritz & Mumford, via the primer], and a field experiment is reported as finding employees given reframing-inclusive training subsequently less likely to jump to conclusions and generating more explanations for problems, per self-report plus peer and supervisor observation shortly after training [Basadur, Graen & Green; Ellspermann, Evans & Basadur, via the primer]. Third, that initial framing typically occurs automatically, and that reminding people of the framing effect increased the creativity of their solutions versus unreminded controls [Reiter-Palmon, Mumford & colleagues, via the primer]. The primer also reports a meta-analytic review as concluding that the problem-finding–creativity relationship is comparable in strength to that of divergent thinking, that problem finding matters more for creativity than intelligence, and that problem finding is heavily under-studied relative to idea generation [Abdulla, Paek, Cramond & Runco, via the primer]. None of these figures or directions of effect should be quoted from this report as verified findings of the named primary studies.

The management-decision echo: a study of 350 decision processes at medium and large companies found more than half failed, often because perceived time pressure cut short examination of the problem from multiple angles [Nutt, Ohio State, cited in Binder & Watkins, HBR, Jan–Feb 2024]: secondhand within the corpus, reached only through the HBR article.

## 4.2 The Wedell-Wedellsborg lineage and its evidence quality

Wedell-Wedellsborg's method, the "slow elevator" lineage, packages reframing as a three-step loop: Frame, Reframe, Move Forward [What's Your Problem?, HBSP, 2020, publisher description]. The canonical example is an illustrative parable, not a documented case: tenants complain the elevator is too slow; the framed solution space is faster motors and better algorithms; the reframe "waiting is boring" yields the mirror by the elevator, which the story credits with resolving the complaints cheaply. No measured outcome for it exists in the corpus [si-labs.com reframing overview, 2026; What's Your Problem? book materials, 2020]. The method's five operating strategies are: look outside the frame, look in the mirror (your own role), rethink the goal, look for positive exceptions, and take others' perspectives [Wedell-Wedellsborg via Next Big Idea Club bookbite, n.d.]. A distinctive methodological stance: reframing is not a long up-front diagnosis but repeated short loops interleaved with action: "using the time you have in a better way" [ibid.; Wedell-Wedellsborg, HBR, 2020].

**Evidence quality, flagged.** The headline figures in this lineage are weakly sourced. The claim that "85 percent of companies said they often struggle to solve the right problems" comes from the author's own survey of 106 C-suite executives from 91 companies across 17 countries, with a companion figure of 87% agreeing this shortcoming carries significant costs [Wedell-Wedellsborg author survey, reported in book marketing copy and si-labs.com, 2026; confidence: low; author-run agreement survey, self-report, no methodology in corpus]. The elevator example, as noted above, is an anecdote appearing "in virtually every design thinking curriculum" [si-labs.com, 2026], not a documented case with measured outcomes. What the lineage claims to inherit is the problem-finding research summarized in §4.1, itself carried only by the author's own primer; what it adds on top (the specific three-step method, the five strategies) has no controlled evaluation anywhere in the corpus.

## 4.3 Frame-restructuring research

Independent of the popular lineage, two research traditions treat frames as objects that can be deliberately restructured. Schön's problem-setting account holds that practitioners do not solve given problems: they frame the problem first, act, observe the situation's "back-talk," and reframe, in an iterative "reflective conversation with the situation" [Schön, 1983, via si-labs.com, 2026; secondary chain, the primary work is not in the corpus and the si-labs link is unverified]. Dorst's frame-creation model, built from studies of expert designers, is a nine-step process focused "not on the generation of solutions but on the ability to create new approaches to the problem situation itself," documented through case studies (retail layouts, club-district crime reframed through a music-festival frame) [Dorst, Frame Innovation, MIT Press, 2015]. Frame creation is a *lateral* move, substituting a different frame, rather than a vertical why-ladder, positioning it as a structured alternative to Five-Whys-style root-cause probing (whose corpus reception is uniformly critical; see the questioning-methods section).

An integrative review of 188 papers spanning management (74), psychology (67), and social science (47) organizes the creative process of problem framing into four constituents (problem representations, activated knowledge, influencing stimuli, and creative logics) and finds the process polychronic: representation-building runs simultaneously with the other three, so framing is an ongoing activity that should be revisited as insight accumulates, not a one-time preparatory step [Magistretti, Pham & Dell'Era, Journal of Product Innovation Management, 2025]. The review also surfaces a conscious-versus-unconscious tension: parts of framing are deliberate and manageable, parts are automatic, consistent with the Mumford-lineage claim, reported only through the method author's primer, that initial framings occur without awareness [Reiter-Palmon et al., via Wedell-Wedellsborg primer, 2025].

The prescriptive management version is Binder and Watkins's E5 process: expand, examine, empathize, elevate, envision [Binder & Watkins, HBR, Jan–Feb 2024]. Its "expand" phase centers on frame-storming (generating many alternative problem frames before any brainstorming of solutions, via "What if…?" and "How might we…?" questions), explicitly aimed at surfacing assumptions and blind spots; its "examine" phase uses the iceberg model to descend from surface events through behavioral patterns and systemic structures to established mental models [ibid.]. E5 is consultant-derived methodology illustrated by a composite company example, not a tested intervention [confidence: low for efficacy claims; the article reports no outcome data].

## 4.4 Assumption surfacing

The corpus's assumption-surfacing techniques cluster into three moves. (1) *Generate-then-test*: frame-storming's instruction to treat every candidate frame as a hypothesis: "rather than make assumptions, generate alternative hypotheses and then test them" [Binder & Watkins, HBR, 2024]. (2) *Excavate*: the iceberg model's deepest layer targets established mental models: in the worked example, the organization's untested conviction that its product was superior and needed no quality checks [ibid.]. (3) *Map against the goal*: the immunity-to-change four-column exercise maps a stated goal against the person's competing commitments and the "big assumptions" sustaining them; its efficacy evidence is one small comparison study in which coached supervisors (n = 45) self-reported more goal progress than comparisons (n = 25), with the four-column map rated the most useful element [Markus, Journal of Applied Behavioral Science, 2013; confidence: low, single study, self-report].

Absence worth recording: the strategy literature's assumption-surfacing apparatus, "what would have to be true" reverse-engineering and strategic choice cascades (the Lafley/Martin lineage), does not appear anywhere in the corpus, so no evidence-based statement about it can be made here.

## 4.5 Opportunity framing versus deficit framing

The corpus supports a modest, not sweeping, version of the claim that framing toward strengths and desired futures outperforms framing toward deficits. The direct experimental evidence is the randomized comparison of question types (N = 246): solution-focused questions (miracle, exception) reduced negative affect more than problem-focused questions, and scaling questions generated more concrete action steps, but the authors' summary was that solution-focused and problem-focused questions "might be more similar than different in their immediate impact" [Neipp, Beyebach, Sanchez-Prada & Delgado Álvarez, "Solution-Focused versus Problem-Focused Questions: Differential Effects of Miracles, Exceptions and Scales", Journal of Family Therapy, 2021]. The "look for positive exceptions" reframing strategy [Wedell-Wedellsborg, 2020] is explicitly borrowed from this solution-focused tradition (the book's index credits Steve de Shazer).

In coaching, the opportunity-framing stance is institutionalized rather than tested: coaching practice is described as heavily influenced by positive psychology, with appreciative inquiry named among its facilitation techniques and strength-based coaching defined as identifying and leveraging existing strengths rather than remediating weaknesses [Cannon-Bowers et al., Frontiers in Psychology, 2023, review section; MacKie, 2014, cited therein]. The corpus contains no dismantling study isolating opportunity-framing versus deficit-framing as the active variable in coaching outcomes, and its only standalone appreciative-inquiry source is a practitioner press release [CWH Institute / RLD Group, EIN Presswire, 2026; promotional, not evidence]. Net: opportunity framing has one supportive RCT on immediate affect and action-step concreteness [Neipp et al., 2021], plus tradition-level endorsement; deficit framing is nowhere shown to be categorically harmful.

## 4.6 Goal decomposition and subgoaling

<!-- editorial:background -->
Goal-systems theory supplies the structural vocabulary for decomposition: goals sit in hierarchies, cognitively linked to means below them; a means may serve a subgoal, which serves an overarching goal. Configurations matter (a means may be shared across goals, and a goal may be reachable through alternative means), and the strength of the association between an activity and its end is itself motivationally consequential.
<!-- /editorial -->

Within this architecture [Kruglanski et al., goal-systems theory, via Woolley & Fishbach, Means-Ends Fusion chapter, Goal Systems Theory, OUP, 2023], the corpus's decomposition evidence runs from classic learning research to modern meta-analysis:

- **Proximal subgoals in learning.** Children (N = 40, ages 7–10) with severe deficits and disinterest in mathematics who pursued self-directed learning under proximal subgoals progressed rapidly, achieved substantial mastery, and developed self-efficacy and intrinsic interest; distal goals had *no demonstrable effects* relative to no goals [Bandura & Schunk, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1981]. Goal proximity also produced more accurate self-knowledge of capability [ibid.].
- **Specificity and proximity generally.** Specific challenging goals outperform vague "do your best" goals, and proximal goals outperform distal ones, effects attributed to feedback and self-monitoring advantages [Locke & Latham, 1990; Bandura & Schunk, 1981; both as reviewed in Gollwitzer, American Psychologist, 1999].
- **Implementation intentions.** Goal intentions alone account for only 20–30% of variance in behavior [Gollwitzer, American Psychologist, 1999]. If-then plans linking an anticipated cue to a response ("If situation Y arises, I will do Z") produced a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = .65) across a 94-study meta-analysis [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006, as reported in Webb & Sheeran, JESP, 2007], with the mechanism experimentally identified as heightened accessibility of the specified cue plus strengthened cue–response links that automate initiation [Webb & Sheeran, JESP, 2007, N = 74 mediation study]. Domain-specific estimates are smaller: physical activity g = 0.31 post-intervention and g = 0.24 at follow-up [Bélanger-Gravel, Godin & Amireault, Health Psychology Review, 2013, via Wang & Gai]; healthy-eating d = 0.51 versus d = 0.29 for reducing unhealthy eating [Adriaanse et al., 2011, via Wang & Gai].
- **Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII).** Combining a desired-future/present-obstacles contrast with if-then planning yields g = 0.336 across 21 studies (24 effect sizes, N = 15,907), stronger when delivered interactively (g = 0.465) than by documents (g = 0.277); the authors note publication bias, so true effects may be smaller [Wang & Gai, Frontiers in Psychology, 2021]. Mental contrasting matters because thinking only about positive future outcomes *decreases* goal-relevant effort [Oettingen & Mayer, 2002, via Wang & Gai, 2021].

## 4.7 When decomposition helps and when it hurts

Decomposition is not neutral scaffolding; three corpus findings mark its failure modes.

**Subgoal framing flips the sign.** In four studies, when people construed success on a single subgoal as *progress*, further actions toward the superordinate goal were treated as substitutes and became less likely; when the same subgoal success was construed as evidence of *commitment* to the superordinate goal, further actions were treated as complements and became more likely [Fishbach, Dhar & Zhang, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2006]. An elicitation or planning tool that celebrates subgoal completion as progress may therefore risk a demotivating substitution effect in settings resembling these studies [confidence: inferential transfer from the cited experiments, not a tested design result].

**Decomposing fused activities carries a theoretical risk.** The means-ends fusion model holds that intrinsic motivation *is* the perceptual fusion of activity and end: single, tightly linked means-goal structures are experienced as more intrinsically motivating than structures where the activity is one of many separated means [Woolley & Fishbach, MEF chapter, OUP, 2023]. Two implications for decomposition, both theoretical design risks rather than demonstrated harms of elicitation: goal-systems theory's "more is less" finding (adding alternative means dilutes motivation toward any single means [Kruglanski, Goal Systems Theory volume, 2023]) and the model's warning that prying an intrinsically motivating activity apart into means and separate ends may undermine the experience rather than clarify it [Woolley & Fishbach, 2023]. Intrinsic motivation is not decoration: it predicted actual goal attainment, including New Year's resolution follow-through [Woolley & Fishbach, 2017, via MEF chapter].

**Boundary conditions on planning interventions.** MCII effects vanished or reversed direction across identifiable moderators: effective in individualist but not collectivist cultures for MOOC completion [Kizilcec & Cohen, 2017, via Wang & Gai, 2021]; effective in autonomy-focused but not highly structured settings for patient exercise [Sailer et al., 2015, ibid.]; effective for hazardous but not non-hazardous drinkers [Wittleder et al., 2019, ibid.]. And self-generated plans, the realistic deployment mode for any dialogue tool, can be built on poorly chosen obstacles or ineffective if-then responses, a quality risk the meta-analysts flag explicitly [Wang & Gai, 2021].

Read together with §4.6's subgoal and planning evidence, a design hypothesis rather than a validated rule, assembled from evidence domains as different as child mathematics learning, implementation-intention meta-analyses, and a theoretical fusion model: decompose for *attainment* when the goal is extrinsic, distal, and stalled (where proximal subgoals and if-then plans have their strongest record [Bandura & Schunk, 1981; Webb & Sheeran, 2007]); avoid or delay decomposition when the pursuit is intrinsically motivated and activity-end fusion may be doing the motivational work [Woolley & Fishbach, 2023]; and wherever subgoals exist, frame their completion as commitment evidence, not progress credit [Fishbach, Dhar & Zhang, 2006]. Each boundary carries the uncertainty of its source domain and none has been tested as a rule for elicitation tools.

# What Actually Works, and How Questioning Goes Wrong

## The process evidence: subtraction beats addition

The most developed process-evidence base on elicitation dialogue in this corpus comes from motivational interviewing (MI), and its central finding is uncomfortable for anyone building a questioning technique: the most consistent signal concerns what is associated with harm, not what helps.

The first meta-analytic test of MI's "technical hypothesis" (therapist skill → client change talk → outcome) pooled 12 primary studies. Therapist MI-consistent skills correlated with more client change talk (r = .26, p < .0001), and MI-*inconsistent* skills (confronting, directing, warning) correlated with less change talk (r = −.17) and more sustain talk. But client change talk did not predict follow-up outcome (r = .06, ns), while sustain talk, the client's language *against* change, predicted worse outcome (r = −.24, p = .001) [Magill et al., Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 2014]. The larger replication (36 studies, N = 3,025) refined rather than reversed this picture: MI-consistent skill correlated with *both* more change talk (r = .55) and more sustain talk (r = .40); the technical hypothesis survived only in proportion terms (proportion MI-consistency → proportion change talk r = .11; proportion change talk → reduced risk behavior r = −.16); sustain talk again independently predicted worse outcome (r = .19 with worse outcome); and the *relational* hypothesis, that empathy and "MI spirit" directly cause change, was not supported in trial data [Magill et al., JCCP, 2018]. An earlier mechanism review reached the compatible conclusion that MI-inconsistent therapist behavior is the most consistent process predictor of poor outcomes [Apodaca & Longabaugh, Addiction, 2009].

Read together, the implication the synthesis calls "harm subtraction" is this: skilled questioning is associated with more pro-change speech, but that speech is a weak independent predictor; the strong, replicated correlational pattern is that directive, fix-it, confrontational behavior goes with more counter-change speech, and counter-change speech tracks worse outcomes. The first-order design recommendation for any elicitor, human or machine, is therefore precautionary: remove the behaviors associated with worse outcomes before perfecting evocation technique [Magill et al., 2014; Magill et al., 2018]. [confidence: high on the correlational pattern; note these are meta-analyses of process correlations, not randomized manipulations of therapist behavior, so the causal reading remains an inference]

Outcome-level MI evidence bounds expectations. Against weak comparators MI produces small durable effects (average g = 0.28, 119 studies); against specific alternative treatments the difference is nonsignificant (g = 0.09) [Lundahl et al., Research on Social Work Practice, 2010]. An earlier meta of 72 trials found a short-term between-group effect of d = 0.77 decaying to 0.30 at one-year follow-ups, with two striking moderators: effects were larger with ethnic-minority populations and *when MI practice was not manual-guided* [Hettema, Steele & Miller, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology, 2005]. The manualization finding is the corpus's clearest warning against scripting elicitation as a fixed flow, though the corpus contains no study that isolates *why* unmanualized delivery did better (responsiveness versus study-level confounds is unresolved; the round-2.5 retrieval on this question returned candidate sources but no adjudicating study).

## Does question type matter as much as assumed?

Less than the branded methods advertise. The corpus's only randomized head-to-head of individual question types comes from solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT): 246 participants described a personal problem and were randomized to problem-focused, miracle, exception, or scaling questions. Miracle and exception questions reduced negative affect more than problem-focused questioning; scaling questions generated more concrete action steps; but the authors' own summary is that "solution-focused and problem-focused questions might be more similar than different in their immediate impact on clients" [Neipp, Beyebach, Sanchez-Prada & Delgado Álvarez, Journal of Family Therapy, 2021]. So question types have real but modest and *specific* effects: future/exception frames move feeling, concreteness frames move planning.

At the package level, SFBT meta-analyses disagree on magnitude: early pooling found small effects (d = 0.13–0.26, significant only for internalizing problems) [Kim, Research on Social Work Practice, 2008], while a recent meta of community-based RCTs reports a medium pooled g = 0.654 across 28 studies and 340 effect sizes [Franklin et al., Research on Social Work Practice, 2024]. [disputed: SFBT effect size ranges from ~0.13 to ~0.65 depending on inclusion criteria and comparator type; treat any single figure with caution.]

The convergent picture across MI and SFBT process work is that *ratios and side effects* matter more than technique counts: the proportion of helpful-to-unhelpful practitioner behaviors predicts client language better than raw frequencies of "good" moves [Magill et al., 2018], and in the one randomized question-type comparison, with undergraduates on a written task and immediate outcomes only, question type shifted which byproduct appeared (affect shift versus action steps) more than overall success [Neipp et al., 2021]. Whether that byproduct pattern holds in ongoing clinical dialogue is untested in this corpus.

## Failure modes with evidence

**The advice-giving reflex ("righting reflex").** Miller and Rollnick's term for the helper's natural impulse to fix: it leads practitioners to become "overly directive and telling a client what to do instead of evoking the client's own motivation and strategies" [SAMHSA TIP 35, Enhancing Motivation for Change, 2019, citing Miller & Rollnick 2013]. The mechanism claim in the training literature is the ambivalence dynamic: MI theory hypothesizes that advocating one side of an ambivalent person's dilemma evokes the other side, so the advisee ends up rehearsing the case for the status quo [SAMHSA TIP 35, 2019; UNODC Treatnet Volume B training materials]. The empirical anchor is the MI process evidence above: MI-inconsistent (directive/confrontational) behavior is the most consistent predictor of sustain talk and worse outcomes [Magill et al., 2014, 2018; Apodaca & Longabaugh, 2009]. Which underlying mechanism carries the effect (psychological reactance, the ambivalence dynamic, or autonomy degradation) is not adjudicated anywhere in the corpus; the round-2.5 deepening pass retrieved reactance-theory and self-determination-theory sources on this question but no study that separates the three accounts.

**Leading and presupposition-loaded questions.** The forensic lineage is the corpus's hardest evidence that question wording alters what people report. Across four experiments with 490 subjects viewing filmed events, questions containing presuppositions, true or false, increased the likelihood that subjects later reported having seen the presupposed object; questions asked right after an event "introduce new, not necessarily correct, information, which is then added to the memorial representation of the event, thereby causing its reconstruction or alteration" [Loftus, Cognitive Psychology, 1975]. Even single-word framing shifts estimates: subjects asked "how tall was the basketball player?" guessed ~79 inches on average versus ~69 inches for "how short?" [Harris, 1973, as reported in Loftus, 1975]. Adult suggestibility varies systematically by leading-question type [Sharman & Powell, Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2012], interrogative pressure and negative feedback shift answers in simulated forensic interviews [Drake, Personality and Individual Differences, 2010, Gudjonsson tradition], and the distortion generalizes beyond the lab-witness setting: a randomized field experiment with older survey respondents (N = 235) found suggestive interviewer behavior in presenting response alternatives and in probing measurably influenced responses, their distributions, and even parameter estimates between variables [Smit, Dijkstra & van der Zouwen, Journal of Official Statistics, 1997, Statistics Sweden-hosted]. Question phrasing also shapes honesty, not just memory: in strategic exchanges, negative-assumption questions (presupposing a problem exists) elicited more truthful disclosure than positive-assumption or general questions, because respondents use wording to infer what the asker knows and expects [Minson, VanEpps, Yip & Schweitzer, OBHDP, 2018]. The design lesson cuts two ways: presuppositions distort recall, yet a well-chosen presupposition can license disclosure. The corpus does not contain a study testing these wording effects in goal-elicitation dialogue specifically; the transfer from witnesses and negotiators to help-seekers is an inference.

**Interviewer-hypothesis contamination.** The strongest single result for anyone (or any model) entering a conversation with a prior about what the other person "really" wants: in a paired design (584 participants randomly assigned to interviewer or witness roles and run as interviewer-witness dyads), witnesses viewed a mock-crime video and were interviewed a week later by interviewers given correct, incorrect, or no information about the event. Blind interviewers elicited more information at no cost to accuracy, replicating earlier findings; critically, *instructions to avoid suggestive questions did not moderate the informed-interviewer penalty*, and the damage extended beyond the interview itself: a week later, in the instructed condition, witnesses of blind interviewers recalled the event more accurately than witnesses of incorrectly informed interviewers, and blind interviewers themselves remembered the witness's account more accurately [Rivard, FIU dissertation, 2014]. [confidence: medium-high; a dissertation, but pre-registered-scale N and consistent with prior published work it cites (Cantlon et al., 1996).] Whether the blind-interviewer advantage transfers to coaching or requirements elicitation is untested in the corpus; the deepening round flagged this as an open replication target.

**Premature convergence and anchoring.** Diagnostic-reasoning experiments give the failure a mechanism and a magnitude. In a within-subject randomized experiment, 58 fourth-year medical students diagnosed 12 clinical vignettes with salient distracting findings placed at the beginning, at the end, or absent. Early biasing information cut diagnostic accuracy 61% (mean 0.11 versus 0.27 without the distractor) and, decisively for mechanism, *reduced* processing time per word, the signature of premature closure rather than of competing hypotheses fighting it out (which would have increased processing time) [Al Essa, Schmidt, Mamede, Knežević, Zwaan & Magzoub, Medical Education, 2026, PubMed 42051011]. The attentional account of why closure persists comes from chess: eye-tracking shows the first idea that comes to mind directs attention toward confirming information and away from inconsistent information, "even when players believe they are looking for alternatives" [Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, Current Directions in Psychological Science, 2010; PLOS ONE eye-movement study, 2013]. Dispositional need for cognitive closure predicts diagnostic shortcut-taking in medical decision making [Roets et al., Medical Decision Making, 2014], and hypothesis generation is measurably constrained by information order and base rates [Weber et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1993]. For dialogue design the transfer claim is direct but, again, untested in conversation: an elicitor's first hypothesis about the user's goal will bias which follow-up questions get asked and which answers get attended to, and merely instructing the elicitor to stay open is exactly the debiasing move that failed in both the interviewing [Rivard, 2014] and Einstellung [Bilalić et al., 2010] literatures.

**Question-behavior / mere-measurement effects.** Corpus coverage here is thin, and this section says so rather than padding. The synthesis reports zero hits for the phenomenon across all 385 round-1 full texts. The round-2.5 deepening pass retrieved on-point titles (a meta-analytic synthesis of the question-behavior effect in Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2016; the Wood et al. review of intention and self-prediction question effects, Personality and Social Psychology Review, 2016; Sprott et al., "The question-behavior effect: what we know and where we go from here"), which establish that the literature exists and is meta-analyzed, but no full text was read into the corpus, so no effect sizes or moderators can be responsibly reported here. The open question the phenomenon poses for goal elicitation is real and unanswered in this corpus: whether merely asking someone about a candidate goal changes their subsequent commitment to it, and in which direction. Any design guidance on this point would be memory, not evidence.

## Stopping criteria: how practitioners decide elicitation is done

The corpus contains taxonomies and observations, not validated rules.

MI's "focusing" process is the field's most explicit treatment of the transition from open exploration to a working agenda: focusing means negotiating *which* behavior or topic the conversation will work on, and "agenda mapping" is the trained tool for doing it collaboratively; the training-tool study frames agenda mapping as helpful precisely for the twin challenges of choosing a focus and knowing when to move on [Gobat et al., European Journal for Person Centered Healthcare, 2018]. This is a practitioner taxonomy with a development study behind it, not an outcome-validated stopping rule.

The observational evidence complicates the idea that elicitation "completes" in an explicit goal agreement at all. Transcript analysis of nine highly experienced therapists' first three sessions found *few* behaviors aimed at agreeing explicit goals; instead, therapists worked to ensure that what happened in therapy was meaningful to the client and to track ambivalence and resistance, so that early sessions ran on hope, motivation, and engagement rather than goal consensus [Oddli & McLeod, European Journal of Psychotherapy & Counselling, 2014]. Conversation-analytic work adds that the opening move itself is loaded: even "what do you expect from therapy?" embeds assumptions that a goal pre-exists, is articulable, and is singular, and patients observably resist those assumptions [Schoeb, Studies in Communication Sciences, 2014]. From coaching, the heterodox position is explicit: goal-*setting* culture is itself the pathology, and practice should favor subtle, client-paced goal *exploration* over early target commitment [Jinks & Dexter, International Journal of Evidence Based Coaching and Mentoring, 2012].

Two evidence fragments gesture at usable transition signals. First, in the one randomized question-type comparison, scaling questions produced more concrete action steps where miracle/exception questions shifted affect [Neipp et al., 2021]; no source in this corpus documents practitioners actually using that contrast as a transition rule, so treating it as a pivot from exploring to planning is a design inference, not an observed practice. Second, the MI process finding that sustain talk carries the outcome signal [Magill et al., 2014, 2018] supports, though no source proposes it directly, monitoring counter-change language as a back-off or stop trigger. Beyond these, the corpus contains no observational coding study of the focus-to-plan transition and no validated criterion for when elicitation is done; the synthesis lists this as an open research question. In the one observational sample available, nine experienced therapists' early sessions, "done" looked less like a state of the goal statement than a state of the relationship: the client is engaged, the work feels meaningful, and ambivalence is on the table rather than resolved [Oddli & McLeod, 2014]. Whether that pattern generalizes beyond those therapists and those early sessions is unknown, and it should not be read as a validated stopping criterion.

<!-- editorial:background -->
The methods covered here come from adjacent but separate research traditions, and this corpus samples each of them differently. The motivational-interviewing sources sampled here include observational coding systems that tag therapist and client utterances, which is why that tradition can support meta-analyses of within-session language in this report. The solution-focused sources treat questions themselves as interventions rather than information-gathering. The forensic questioning sources run experiments against verifiable ground truth (a filmed event), which the therapeutic literatures lack. The diagnostic-reasoning sources on premature closure use clinical vignettes with objectively correct answers. Transfer of findings across these traditions into everyday goal-elicitation dialogue is therefore typically an argument by analogy, strongest where the mechanism is general (attention, memory, ambivalence) and weakest where outcomes depend on domain-specific ground truth.

# Design Implications for an AI Goal-Clarification Dialogue Partner

<!-- editorial:background -->
Separate research communities bear directly on this design problem, and the papers gathered in this corpus treat them separately. The trials community builds and tests conversational agents that deliver coaching or motivational-interviewing dialogues to real users and measures behavioral outcomes. The clarification community studies when and how large language models should ask clarifying questions, treating the user's goal as a hidden variable to be located. In the papers selected here, the trials work tends to model the goal as something transformed by good questioning, while the clarification work tends to model it as fixed and merely underspecified; these are modeling tendencies visible in the selected papers, not characterizations of either community as a whole. A workable design has to draw on both, and on the human-elicitation evidence from earlier sections, because neither body of work alone covers the full loop from vague want to committed plan.
<!-- /editorial -->

## 1. What the trials show: automated goal dialogues work, but the effect does not live where the branding says

The headline result is parity on the formulaic core of coaching. An AI chatbot coach ("Vici," built on the GROW model) matched human coaches on client goal attainment across paired longitudinal randomized controlled trials run over 10 months, with both beating control groups; the authors' own interpretation is a warning rather than a triumph, that AI "could replace human coaches who use simplistic, model-based coaching approaches" [Terblanche, Molyn, de Haan & Nilsson, PLOS ONE, 2022]. This predates modern LLMs, so the parity was achieved by a scripted agent on a scripted method.

LLM-era trials extend this. A GPT-4 motivational-interviewing (MI) chatbot increased users' readiness to change in a pre-registered RCT; generating text under MI principles measurably reduced potential harms in outputs; and outcomes depended heavily on user behavior type (cooperative, reflective, pre-informed) [Meyer & Elsweiler, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 2025]. A 12-week automated MI chatbot integrated into routine primary care (pragmatic multicentre RCT, 627 randomized, 460 analyzed) improved physical activity by 576 MET-min/week plus diet and committed action at 12 weeks, sustained at 9-month follow-up, with a dose-response pattern among adherent users and no serious adverse events reported [Wong et al., npj Digital Medicine, 2026]. An MI-style chatbot outperformed a neutral one on engagement and therapeutic alliance in smoking cessation (n = 153) [He et al., BMC Public Health, 2022], and the fine-tuned generative agent Therabot produced large symptom reductions (d ≈ 0.63–0.90) in a 4-week RCT (N = 210) with user-rated alliance comparable to human therapists [Heinz et al., NEJM AI, 2025]. A scoping review of AI-delivered MI catalogues broad feasibility but flags that MI *fidelity* measurement in these systems remains unresolved [Karve et al., JMIR, 2025].

Among the automated-chatbot trials in this corpus, exactly one is a true factorial component-dismantling trial, a design that switches individual components on and off across arms (the human-methods sections describe randomized question-type and module comparisons, but not factorial dismantling of a deployed system), and its result is deflationary for goal-dialogue enthusiasts. An optimization RCT (N = 205, 2^4 factorial) decomposed an eating-disorders services-uptake chatbot into psychoeducation, motivational interviewing, personalized recommendations, and repeated administration (check-ins/reminders). Only repeated administration significantly increased the primary outcome, mental health services use. The MI component made the chatbot feel more helpful but was associated with *larger declines* in attitudes toward change over time; psychoeducation did nothing [Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., International Journal of Eating Disorders, 2024]. In the one place a goal-dialogue component was isolated experimentally, persistence and follow-up carried the outcome, not the clever conversation. [confidence: single trial, one clinical domain; the MI component here targeted service uptake, not goal clarification per se]

One further trial speaks to adaptive structure: a motivation-aware LLM coaching framework that classifies user utterances with MI's change-talk/sustain-talk coding and routes low-motivation users to rapport and support while routing action-ready users to planning strategies significantly increased readiness to change versus a planning-only baseline (140 participants, single session), was rated more MI-adherent (less unpermitted persuasion, no unsolicited advice), and, in the qualitative analysis, users who *agreed with the agent on the change plan* showed the greatest gains in intrinsic motivation [An LLM-Based Motivation-Aware Framework for AI Coaching for Behaviour Change, Proceedings of CHI 2026; the retrieved record for this paper carries no author list, so it is cited by title as "Motivation-Aware Framework, CHI 2026" throughout].

**Design inference** (from Fitzsimmons-Craft et al. 2024 and Wong et al. 2026's dose-response): a goal-clarification agent should be architected around return visits and follow-through scaffolding as first-class features, not as an afterthought to a single brilliant clarification session. Repeated contact is the most promising single lever in the trials that speak to it, but the base is one component trial in one clinical domain plus a dose-response association in another; the weighting needs replication before it generalizes across techniques or domains.

## 2. The LLM-specific evidence: models under-ask, and human imitation is the wrong teacher

The clarification literature converges on a consistent baseline failure. Across ten LLMs evaluated on 6,120 multi-turn dialogues built from a five-dimensional ambiguity taxonomy and six simulated user personas, models show a systematic **under-clarification bias**: they answer prematurely rather than ask, and performance degrades as dialogue depth increases and as users reply vaguely, contradictorily, or off-topic [Luo et al., ClarifyMT-Bench, arXiv 2512.21120]. Because current assistants are optimized for helpfulness and fluency, confident answers to underspecified requests are the default failure mode, not the exception [Luo et al., ClarifyMT-Bench].

Clarification is increasingly framed as a *sequential policy problem*, not a question-generation problem: whether to ask, what to ask, when to stop, when to commit. RegretBench evaluates whole dialogues against a reference policy under hidden intent and finds that models with similar final accuracy differ substantially in efficiency, robustness to user behavior, and stopping decisions; a plausible-sounding question that fails to distinguish the live candidate intents scores as waste [Ta et al., RegretBench, arXiv 2607.21143]. Training-side fixes in the corpus include: preference-labeling on simulated *future* turns rather than the current turn (+5% F1 on when-to-ask decisions) [Zhang, Knox & Choi, arXiv 2410.13788]; self-improvement loops that reward questions raising the likelihood of an oracle user's eventual response (72% win rate after two iterations) [Andukuri et al., STaR-GATE, 2024]; information-gain rewards over Bayesian belief updates toward the true goal (+3.7% task success for +0.3 turns of added interaction) [Deng et al., arXiv 2606.03135]; rubric-guided reinforcement learning with benchmarks that separate intent-deficient from false-premise queries [Zhao, Fang & Cheng, AskBench, Findings of ACL 2026]; and an upstream "intention-refinement" specialist (Mistral-Interact) that converts vague instructions into explicit actionable goals before an agent executes anything [Qian et al., "Tell Me More!", ACL 2024].

The most consequential negative result: **model uncertainty does not mirror human clarification-seeking behavior**. Using human clarification questions as supervision for when to ask is therefore misaligned with what the model actually needs to resolve; asking when the *model's own* uncertainty is high significantly improved task success over human-imitation timing [Testoni & Fernández, EACL 2024].

**Design inference** (from Testoni & Fernández 2024 and Deng et al.): a goal-clarification agent should trigger its probes off its own live uncertainty over candidate goals, not off a script of "the questions a good coach asks at this stage." The human-methods literature supplies the *form* and *hygiene* of questions; the model's uncertainty state should supply the *timing*.

**Design inference** (from ClarifyMT-Bench's depth-degradation finding and RegretBench's cost-of-asking framing): long clarification sessions are where the failure risk concentrates, so the agent needs an explicit turn budget and a monitored stopping rule rather than open-ended exploration.

## 3. Design levers, in priority order

The following levers synthesize this section's trial evidence with the mechanism evidence from earlier sections. Each is tagged as evidenced or as design inference from named findings.

**3.1 Harm-subtraction is the first design rule.** The most consistent negative correlate in the MI process evidence, the strongest replicated pattern in the human material this report draws on, is this: MI-inconsistent behavior (confronting, directing, unsolicited advice, the "righting reflex") predicts more sustain talk and worse outcomes across both process meta-analyses, while adding MI-consistent skill produces change talk that fails to predict outcomes as an independent effect [Magill et al., JCCP, 2014; Magill et al., 2018]; MI training doctrine holds that advocating one side of an ambivalent person's dilemma evokes the other side [SAMHSA TIP 35, 2019]. These are process correlations, not randomized manipulations of elicitor behavior. Expert human coaches interviewed for LLM-coach design independently converge on the same rule: facilitative not prescriptive, no unsolicited advice, no advising through assumptions [Jörke et al., expert interviews, as reported in the Motivation-Aware Framework paper, CHI 2026]. And the Meyer & Elsweiler trial found MI-style generation's clearest contribution was *mitigating harms* in LLM output [Meyer & Elsweiler, IJHCS, 2025]. **Design inference** (precautionary, from the Magill 2014/2018 correlational pattern and Meyer & Elsweiler 2025): specify the agent first by its prohibitions (no premature advice, no siding with change, no fixing) and verify those in evaluation before investing in evocation technique. Subtraction as the floor and technique as the ceiling is the inference's working slogan, not a measured ranking.

**3.2 Sequencing: reflect before probing, probe before reframing, and match question type to intended product.** Experienced therapists do little explicit early goal negotiation; they establish meaningfulness and track ambivalence first [Oddli & McLeod, European Journal of Psychotherapy & Counselling, 2014]. The only randomized head-to-head of question types found specific, modest effects: miracle/exception questions shifted affect, scaling questions produced concrete action steps, and solution- versus problem-focused questions were otherwise "more similar than different" [Neipp et al., Journal of Family Therapy, 2021]. Experiential engagement beats rationale-giving in values work [Levin et al., Behavior Therapy, 2012]. **Design inference** (from Oddli & McLeod 2014, Neipp et al. 2021): sequence the dialogue as reflect/validate → evocative probe → (only if the goal frame itself is failing) lateral reframe; select question type by the product needed at that moment, affect shift versus concrete next step, rather than by school loyalty. **Design inference** (from Card's Five Whys critique [BMJ Quality & Safety, 2017] and Dorst's frame-creation method [MIT Press, 2015]): when probing stalls, prefer lateral frame substitution over deeper "why" chains; vertical laddering is the documented failure mode, not the safe default.

**3.3 Presupposition-load ("cleanness") checks on every emitted question.** Questions carrying false or unexamined presuppositions measurably distort what people subsequently report [Loftus, Cognitive Psychology, 1975], and even innocuous openers like "what do you expect from therapy?" embed assumptions (a goal exists, is formed, is singular) that clients observably resist [Schoeb, Studies in Communication Sciences, 2014]. Clean Language supplies a fully specified minimal-presupposition question grammar, and a quantitative "cleanness rating" for questions has been proposed [Small & Ey, Naturalistic Decision Making conference, 2024]. Interviewers holding prior information elicit less than blind interviewers even under debiasing instructions [Rivard, FIU dissertation, 2014]. **Design inference** (from Loftus 1975, Schoeb 2014, Small & Ey 2024): run a pre-emission filter that scores candidate questions for presupposition load and either strips the presupposition or flags it as a deliberate, marked hypothesis ("I might be wrong, but it sounds like..."). This is directly implementable: an LLM can be its own cleanness scorer, and no source in the corpus has tested the combination. **Design inference** (from Rivard 2014): when the agent has formed a strong prior about the user's "real" goal, that is precisely when its questions need the strictest cleanness screening, because informed questioners demonstrably contaminate elicitation.

**3.4 Sustain-talk-style back-off signals.** Client language *against* change, not language for it, carries the outcome signal (sustain talk → worse outcome, r = −.24 in the first meta-analysis; independently predictive in the second) [Magill et al., 2014; Magill et al., 2018]. The Motivation-Aware Framework (CHI 2026) already demonstrates the enabling capability: real-time MI-coding of user utterances into change/sustain expressions with commitment strength, used to route strategy [Motivation-Aware Framework, CHI 2026]. **Design inference** (from Magill 2018 plus the Motivation-Aware Framework (CHI 2026) routing demonstration): treat rising sustain-talk proportion as the agent's primary back-off trigger, shifting from probing to reflection or explicitly offering to drop the thread. No trial has yet tested sustain-talk monitoring as a *stopping* criterion; it is the corpus's most direct unexploited opening.

**3.5 Stopping criteria and the focus-to-plan transition.** The LLM literature establishes that stopping is a first-class decision with measurable cost on both sides (asking too long burns user tolerance; answering early misreads intent) [Ta et al., RegretBench; Luo et al., ClarifyMT-Bench]. On the human side, MI's "focusing" process has a training taxonomy, agenda mapping, for negotiating which topic to work on [Gobat et al., European Journal for Person Centered Healthcare, 2018], but the corpus contains no observational coding of how expert practitioners actually decide to stop eliciting and move to planning; a targeted round-2.5 retrieval on this question returned no direct evidence, so it remains an open gap [as of: 2026-08, this corpus]. The Motivation-Aware Framework trial (CHI 2026) offers the nearest evidence: routing action-ready users into planning improved perceived planning effectiveness, and plan *agreement* marked the best motivational outcomes [Motivation-Aware Framework, CHI 2026]. **Design inference** (from RegretBench, Gobat et al. 2018, Motivation-Aware Framework CHI 2026): implement the transition as an explicit, user-visible move (a candidate-goal summary offered for confirmation, agenda-mapping style) triggered by the conjunction of low model uncertainty over the goal and low sustain talk, with a hard turn ceiling as backstop. Once past the transition, switch toolkits: implementation-intention and mental-contrasting structures carry the planning-phase evidence (MCII g = 0.336, stronger delivered interactively) [Wang & Gai, Frontiers in Psychology, 2021], with the caveat that subgoal framing as progress rather than commitment can demotivate [Fishbach, Dhar & Zhang, 2006].

**3.6 Flexible policy over rigid script.** MI produced *larger* effects when practiced without a manual [Hettema, Steele & Miller, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology, 2005], and the scripted-GROW parity result is explicitly read by its authors as evidence that rigid model-based coaching is the mechanizable, lowest-value tier [Terblanche et al., 2022]. On the machine side, in the ClarifyMT-Bench evaluation the best-performing agent design there tested (ClarifyAgent) is structured but adaptive, decomposing the problem into perception, persona forecasting, ambiguity tracking, and planning rather than following a fixed question sequence [Luo et al., ClarifyAgent, in ClarifyMT-Bench]. The unmanualized-MI moderator could be confounded by systematic differences in unmanualized studies, and the corpus does not adjudicate; "manualization hurts" is therefore a working hypothesis, not a verdict. **Design inference** (from Hettema 2005 and Terblanche 2022, on that hypothesis): build the agent as a policy with wide discretion over a small set of well-specified moves and hard prohibitions, not as a state machine walking a canonical question list.

**3.7 Concordance-before-selection.** Having people rate candidate goals for autonomous versus controlled motivation *before* choosing among them led to selecting more value-matched goals, mediated by the ratings themselves [Sheldon, Prentice & Osin, Journal of Research in Personality, 2019], and self-concordant goals attract more sustained effort and better well-being on attainment [Sheldon & Elliot, JPSP, 1999]. The Motivation-Aware Framework (CHI 2026) finding that plan agreement predicted the largest intrinsic-motivation gains is convergent trial-side evidence that the user's endorsement of the selected direction matters [Motivation-Aware Framework, CHI 2026]. **Design inference** (from Sheldon, Prentice & Osin 2019): before locking a goal, the agent should elicit several candidate formulations and probe each for autonomous motivation ("would you still want this if no one ever knew?"), turning a questionnaire manipulation into a dialogue stage. Untested as dialogue, cheap to implement, grounded in a randomized result.

**3.8 User-type routing.** User behavior type (cooperative, reflective, pre-informed) significantly moderated chatbot outcomes [Meyer & Elsweiler, IJHCS, 2025]; coaching benefits concentrate in identifiable subgroups (high openness, avoid-goal orientation, low core self-evaluations) [Jones, Woods & Zhou, 2019, controlled field experiment]; MI-style support can be counterproductive for already-motivated clients, motivating readiness-tailored strategy [Motivation-Aware Framework, CHI 2026]; and persona inference improved clarification robustness in the benchmark setting [Luo et al., ClarifyAgent]. **Design inference** (from Meyer & Elsweiler 2025 and Motivation-Aware Framework CHI 2026): classify the user early on two axes, engagement style and readiness, and route: pre-informed/action-ready users get brief confirmation plus planning; reflective users get depth; resistant or ambivalent users get reflection with strict harm-subtraction. No end-to-end trial of such a routing policy exists yet.

## 4. What the evidence does not yet support

Three claims a designer might want are unsupported in this corpus. First, no trial dismantles the *clarification* component from accountability/monitoring components in the successful chatbot RCTs; the one chatbot component trial available in this corpus found the follow-up component, not the MI dialogue, carried the primary outcome [Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., 2024]. Second, safety evidence for prolonged automated probing of values and deeper wants is thin: the trials report harm mitigation in outputs [Meyer & Elsweiler, 2025] and no serious adverse events reported [Wong et al., 2026], which is not the same thing as demonstrated safety, and the corpus contains no study of rumination, destabilized commitments, or dropout caused by deep goal-probing at scale [as of: 2026-08]. Third, there is no controlled comparison of any of the levers above against each other in an AI setting; the ranking offered here is an inference structure over converging evidence, not a measured league table.
